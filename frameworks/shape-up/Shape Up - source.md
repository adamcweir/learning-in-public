# What is Shape-up?
- A way of running a software development team that is bounded by time invested into a project or goal, rather than chunking the project or goal up into seemingly never ending sprint cycles. 
- The time to develop and finish the project is fixed (traditionally six weeks) so the scope is variable to what the team decides is important enough and possible to fit into that time period.
- It was created by the people at basecamp.com, specifically Jason Fried

# Key Tenets of Shape Up
- Don’t start unless you can see the end - Too often projects start vague, so they never end or meander around. Strike the right balance between abstract and concrete, and know where you are headed, and what you hope to end with.
- How do you know when it is shaped? If at the end, the technical person knows what to build. 
- The role of the product manager is to shape the single idea and continue to cut down scope until just the most important golden nuggets remain
- Take what was shaped and translate into 9 boxes of implementation. This helps bucket the project into 9 distinct phases for shared understanding, communication, and progress tracking

# Key Rituals and Artifacts
- Shaping
	- Meant to fit into the sweet spot of concrete and abstract. PRDs can be too abstract, wireframes can be too concrete. And now in the world of AI prototypes, they can be overly prescriptive and fein confidence in a certain direction or approach where there is actually still hidden complexity and uncertainty. 
	- To do it, you need to get 2-3 collaborators together. It helps to have someone technical, someone with design sense, and a strategic thinker in the room
	- SHAPING STEPS
		- **Set boundaries.** First we figure out how much time the raw idea is worth and how to define the problem. This gives us the basic boundaries to shape into.
		- **Rough out the elements.** Then comes the creative work of sketching a solution. We do this at a higher level of abstraction than wireframes in order to move fast and explore a wide enough range of possibilities. The output of this step is an idea that solves the problem within the appetite but without all the fine details worked out.
		- **Address risks and rabbit holes.** Once we think we have a solution, we take a hard look at it to find holes or unanswered questions that could trip up the team. We amend the solution, cut things out of it, or specify details at certain tricky spots to prevent the team from getting stuck or wasting time.
		- **Write the pitch**. Once we think we’ve shaped it enough to potentially bet on, we package it with a formal write-up called a `pitch`. The pitch summarizes the problem, constraints, solution, rabbit holes, and limitations. The pitch goes to the `betting table` for consideration. If the project gets chosen, the pitch can be re-used at kick-off to explain the project to the team.
- Pitch 
	- This is the main artifact of a project. The pitch is a concise but complete (1-2 page) document that includes the problem (the idea or use case that is motivating us to work on it), the appetite (how much time we want to spend on it), the solution, rabbit holes (details about the solution worth calling out to avoid), and no-gos (things that are out of scope)

- Betting
	- Instead of carrying backlogs around, pitches are chosen from a betting table. Everyone gets a chance to review the ideas presented, and chooses which they want to bet a certain amount of time on for the upcoming cycle. This is held during the cool-down session between cycles. The outcome of this meeting is an agreed upon *bet*, which implies commitment and payout. We are looking for something meaningful as a result, and we are committing to give the project the full time of the cycle. As best as possible, you honor the bets that are being made. 
- Building
	- The team then builds for the duration of the cycle. This is typically six weeks. Six weeks is chosen because it is long enough to build something meaningful, but short enough to see the end from the beginning. If the project is not done by the end of the cycle, work on it ends by default. In order for it to continue, it would have to go to the betting table again and be chosen. 
- Cool-down
	- Following each cycle, the team has a two week cool down period. There is no scheduled work, and people are free to work on what they want. This might be tackling tech debt, explore new ideas, or look into new technical possibilities. 

# Expected Benefits
- Relieve the pain of never ending projects, because there are never set end dates to them. 
- Allow product managers to think more strategically about the product
