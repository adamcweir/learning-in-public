# System Traps

## Explanation

A system trap is a structure that reliably produces bad outcomes regardless of who is inside it. The people are competent, the incentives are individually rational, everyone is trying, and the result is still bad — because the structure, not the participants, is generating the behavior.

From Donella Meadows: *"Systems with similar feedback structures produce similar dynamic behaviors, even though they might seem to be very different."*

That similarity is what makes traps worth learning as a set. There is a small number of them, they recur across wildly different domains, and each one has a known way out that is different from the intuitive response. The intuitive response usually deepens the trap — which is the most reliable signal that you're in one.

The diagnostic value is high because traps are identifiable by symptom before you understand the mechanism. If your fixes get neutralized, if a shared thing keeps degrading while everyone behaves reasonably, if standards are lower than they were and nobody can say when that happened, if a metric is improving while the thing it stands for gets worse — you can name the trap from the outside and work backward to the structure.

The four below are the ones that account for most of what goes wrong in software organizations.

## In Tech Contexts

### Policy Resistance

**The mechanism.** Multiple actors pull a system state toward different targets. Each pulls harder as the state drifts from their target. The result is a system held in place under enormous tension — and any single actor who pushes harder is met with a compensating pull from everyone else. Effort rises across the board while the state stays put. When the pushing actor gives up or leaves, the system snaps to wherever the remaining forces put it, which is often worse than the starting point.

**In a software org.** Engineering pushes for refactoring time. Product pushes for feature commitments. Engineering wins a "20% tech debt allocation," Product responds by increasing the number of committed features, and the allocation gets spent on debt directly blocking those features. Nothing structural improves and both sides now believe the other negotiated in bad faith. The same shape appears with quality gates that generate workarounds, with mandated processes that get performed rather than followed, and with hiring bars that erode under headcount pressure.

**The way out.** Let go of the rope and find the goal all parties can actually pursue. This works because the tension is generated by conflicting targets, so removing a target removes the force. The move is to name each actor's real objective out loud — Product's is predictable delivery of things customers want, Engineering's is the ability to keep delivering next year — and then define a shared target that both objectives serve, such as sustained delivery rate measured over four quarters rather than features per sprint. Measurement has to follow the new goal, or the old targets keep operating underneath the new language.

### Tragedy of the Commons

**The mechanism.** A resource is shared, the benefit of consuming it is private and immediate, and the cost of depleting it is distributed and delayed. Each actor's rational calculation says consume. The feedback from the resource's condition back to the individual decision is weak or absent, so the resource is drawn down until it fails.

**In a software org.** The shared codebase is the canonical instance: each team ships faster by taking a shortcut, and the resulting complexity is paid by everyone later, including teams that took no shortcuts. The on-call rotation is another: every team benefits from shipping an alert that pages a human, and the cost lands on whoever is holding the pager that week. Also: shared CI capacity, shared staging environments, the design system, and the meeting calendars of a few senior people whom everyone can invite at no cost to themselves.

**The way out.** Restore the missing feedback between the actor and the resource. Three ways, in ascending order of cost: make the consumption visible and attributed, so the cost is at least social; assign ownership of a section so the depleter and the payer are the same team; or regulate access with an enforced rule. Attribution is usually enough and is the cheapest — a dashboard showing pages generated per team, or complexity added per team, changes behavior faster than a policy, because the trap runs on invisibility rather than on malice.

### Drift to Low Performance

**The mechanism.** This is the most underrated trap in software, and the one most likely to be operating in your org right now without a single person having noticed.

The structure is a balancing loop whose goal is set by recent performance. You measure how you're doing against how you were doing lately. When performance dips, the perceived standard dips with it, which lowers the effort required to close the gap, which permits a further dip. Bad news is also weighted more heavily than good — a miss reads as evidence about what's realistic, while a beat reads as luck. So the standard ratchets in one direction only.

Nothing about this feels like decline while it happens. Every individual step is small, defensible, and made by reasonable people under real pressure. The comparison is always against last month, and against last month you are fine. The drift is only visible against a fixed external reference, and organizations rarely keep one.

**In a software org.** Code review is where it shows most clearly: substantive reviews with five or six comments become two comments, then "LGTM" within ninety seconds of the PR opening. No one decided to stop reviewing. Each individual approval was reasonable given the deadline. The same shape hits incident response — the one-hour SLA that becomes four hours because the last several took four hours and nothing terrible happened. It hits test coverage, where the "we don't test this kind of thing" category quietly expands. It hits interview bars during a hiring push, where each individual candidate is defensible relative to the last few hires rather than relative to the bar you set. And it hits meetings, where agendas and written decisions decay into recurring status calls.

The recognizable moment is an artifact from eighteen months ago — an old design doc, an old postmortem, an old PR thread — that looks unrecognizably rigorous compared to today's. That reaction is the diagnosis.

**The way out.** Anchor the standard to something that cannot drift, and never to recent performance. Two structural moves. First, set the standard absolutely: "review comments must engage with the design, not just style" or "P1 acknowledgment within one hour," written down with a date, and evaluated against the written version rather than against the trend. Second, invert the asymmetry deliberately — treat the best recent performance as the reference point and the bad results as the anomalies. Meadows' formulation is to make the perceived standard depend on best performance rather than worst. A team that benchmarks against its best quarter drifts upward with exactly the same loop that otherwise drifts it down.

### Seeking the Wrong Goal

**The mechanism.** A system delivers on the goal it is actually given. When the measurable proxy diverges from the real objective, the system optimizes the proxy with complete fidelity and the real objective degrades — while every dashboard reports success. The more effort and ingenuity in the system, the faster the divergence, because effort is what makes the optimization efficient.

**In a software org.** Velocity measured in story points produces point inflation and split tickets rather than more delivered value. Test coverage as a target produces assertion-free tests that execute lines. Time-to-first-response as a support metric produces fast acknowledgments and slow resolutions. Number of incidents produces reclassification of incidents. Interview volume as a recruiting metric produces loops for candidates nobody intends to hire. In each case the metric improves, the people are behaving exactly as instructed, and the thing the metric was invented to represent gets worse.

**The way out.** Measure the outcome rather than the activity, and accept a noisier, slower metric to get one that can't be gamed apart from the goal. Where the true outcome is genuinely unmeasurable, pair the proxy with a counter-metric that captures what gaming it would break — coverage paired with escaped defect rate, response time paired with time-to-resolution, velocity paired with cycle time on customer-visible work. Rotating proxies periodically also helps, because a metric's gameability grows with its age.

## Diagnostic Questions

1. **Pull an artifact from eighteen to twenty-four months ago — a code review thread, a design doc, an incident postmortem — and compare it to this week's equivalent. Is today's visibly thinner?** If yes, you're in drift to low performance, and no one in the org will have noticed, because the comparison everyone actually makes is against last month.

2. **What is the standard measured against — a written absolute, or recent results?** If the answer is "we compare to the last few sprints," the drift structure is live whether or not it has drifted yet. This question predicts the trap before the symptom appears.

3. **List the last three initiatives that were adopted and then quietly stopped working. What pushed back, and what was it protecting?** Sustained, distributed resistance is policy resistance and tells you there's an unnamed competing goal. One-off failure to adopt is an execution problem instead.

4. **Which resources are used by everyone and owned by no one?** Name them: the codebase, the alert budget, CI minutes, staging, a specific senior engineer's calendar. Anything on that list that has been degrading is a commons, and the degradation rate tells you how weak the feedback to consumers is.

5. **For your top metric, describe how a smart, well-intentioned person would improve it without improving anything real. Has that already happened?** If the answer is easy to produce and matches what you observe, you're seeking the wrong goal. If you can't construct the gap, the metric is probably fine.

6. **When people behave badly here, is it the same bad behavior regardless of who it is?** Consistent behavior across different people points at structure. Behavior that changes when the person changes points at a personnel issue, and no trap intervention will help.

## Where I'd Start

**I'd test for drift to low performance first, by pulling artifacts from eighteen to twenty-four months ago and comparing them to this week's, before diagnosing anything else.**

The system logic: drift operates by making its own reference point unavailable, so it is the only trap of the four that is invisible from inside the org's normal conversations — and a time-shifted artifact restores the reference point directly.

I start here for two reasons beyond that. It is cheap: an afternoon in git history, the incident log, and the doc archive. And it is frequently comorbid — a commons degrades faster once standards have drifted, and a wrong-goal metric drifts its own targets downward over time. Establishing whether drift is present changes how you read everything else.

**Time-to-signal:** the diagnosis lands the same day. Correction is slow — expect one to two quarters before a re-anchored standard shows up in outcomes, because the loop that drifted down has to run in reverse for a while.

**Cost I'm accepting:** the finding is uncomfortable and lands on people who did nothing individually wrong. Presenting it badly turns a structural observation into a performance conversation and buys resistance. I'd present the artifacts and let the comparison speak, without naming individuals.

**The branch:** if the artifacts show standards have held steady, drift is ruled out and the question becomes which of the other three you have. If specific initiatives keep getting neutralized after adoption, go to policy resistance and spend the next week finding out what goal is pulling the other way — before proposing any fix, because a fix proposed into an unnamed conflict is the thing that gets neutralized. If the degrading thing is shared and unowned, go to tragedy of the commons and start with attribution.

**What I would not start with:** writing a new standards document or running an exhortation campaign. Both traps that respond to exhortation respond only briefly, because the structure that produced the behavior is untouched and reasserts itself in weeks. I also would not start with a policy change under policy resistance — a new policy pushed into a tension system adds force and produces exactly the compensating pull that makes the trap self-sustaining.

## Intervention Levers

### 1. Anchor the standard to something that cannot drift

*For drift to low performance.*

**Why it works:** The drift loop runs on a goal that floats with recent performance. Fix the goal to an external, dated, written reference and the loop has nothing to ratchet against — the same balancing loop now pulls performance back up toward the anchor instead of following it down.

**Strategy:** Write the standard down with a date. Evaluate against the written version, never against the trend. Then set the reference to best recent performance rather than average or worst, which inverts the ratchet direction.

**Tech example:** Review quality has decayed to rubber-stamping. The team writes down what a review must engage with — design fit, failure modes, test adequacy — dates it, and picks the strongest review from the last quarter as the reference example. Monthly, they sample five merged PRs and compare against the written standard rather than against last month's samples. The comparison target stops moving, so the standard stops moving with it.

### 2. Surface the opposing goal before proposing a fix

*For policy resistance.*

**Why it works:** Resistance is generated by a target you haven't named. Pushing adds force to a system already in tension and produces an equal compensating pull. Naming the opposing target converts a tug-of-war into a negotiable design problem.

**Strategy:** Before proposing anything, get each actor to state what they're protecting and what happens to them if it's lost. Then look for a goal that serves both. If no such goal exists, you have a real tradeoff to decide explicitly rather than a resistance problem to solve.

**Tech example:** Refactoring initiatives keep dying. Instead of negotiating a bigger allocation, engineering asks what Product is protecting and hears "commit dates I can give customers." The shared goal becomes delivery predictability, measured as variance between committed and actual dates. Refactoring the components that cause the most estimate variance now serves Product's target directly, and the resistance disappears because the pull is no longer in opposition.

### 3. Attribute consumption of the shared resource

*For tragedy of the commons.*

**Why it works:** The commons depletes because the feedback from resource condition to individual decision is missing. Restoring visibility restores the feedback without requiring ownership changes or enforcement machinery, which is why it's the cheapest lever that actually works.

**Strategy:** Instrument who consumes and publish it by team. Attribution before ownership, ownership before regulation — escalate only if the cheaper step fails.

**Tech example:** Alert fatigue is degrading on-call. Rather than a new alerting policy, the org publishes a weekly dashboard of pages generated per owning team, with a column for pages that resulted in no action. Teams that were exporting cost onto the rotation can now see it, and so can everyone else. Page volume falls substantially before any rule is written, because the trap was running on invisibility.

### 4. Give the shared resource an owner

*For tragedy of the commons, when attribution alone doesn't hold.*

**Why it works:** Ownership makes the actor who depletes the resource the same actor who pays for the depletion. The private benefit and the distributed cost collapse into one balance sheet, and the individually rational choice changes on its own.

**Strategy:** Partition the resource so each piece has exactly one owning team, and make the owner's own work depend on the condition of their piece. Ownership without that dependency is a name on a wiki page.

**Tech example:** A shared service degrades because five teams contribute to it and none maintains it. Ownership moves to the team whose on-call rotation carries its pages. Contribution stays open to everyone, and the owning team reviews changes against their own operational burden. Quality stabilizes because the reviewer now pays for bad decisions personally.

### 5. Pair every proxy metric with its counter-metric

*For seeking the wrong goal.*

**Why it works:** A single proxy can be optimized away from the goal. A pair chosen so that gaming one degrades the other makes the divergence self-revealing, which is the only durable defense when the true outcome is hard to measure directly.

**Strategy:** For each key metric, write down how a competent person would improve it without helping the real objective, then pick a second metric that would visibly worsen if that happened. Report the pair together, always.

**Tech example:** Velocity in story points is driving inflation. It gets paired with cycle time on customer-visible changes. Splitting tickets to inflate points now raises cycle time, so the pair moves in opposite directions the moment gaming starts and the divergence appears on the same chart. The team keeps the familiar metric and loses the ability to game it quietly.

## What Would Change This Diagnosis

- **The behavior changes when the people change.** Traps produce the same behavior from whoever is in the seat. If a new manager or new team member reverses the pattern within weeks, the structure was not driving it and this lens will send you looking for a mechanism that isn't there.
- **The problem accelerates on its own rather than settling into a stable bad state.** Traps hold systems at a poor equilibrium; compounding decline is a reinforcing loop running. Read [Feedback Loops](feedback-loops.md).
- **Your interventions work, just far later than expected, and you keep abandoning them early.** That reads as resistance and is actually latency. Read [Delays](delays.md) before concluding you're being opposed.
- **Effort produces nothing no matter where you apply it, and no one is pushing back.** No opposing force means no trap — you're probably improving a non-binding input. Read [System Limits](limits.md).
- **Each team's behavior is locally optimal and there's no shared resource or floating standard involved.** That's structural goal conflict between levels rather than a trap. Read [Hierarchy & Suboptimization](hierarchy.md).
- **The system recovers on its own once pressure lifts.** A trap holds after the pressure ends; recovery indicates you're looking at strain and absorptive capacity. Read [Resilience](resilience.md).

## Summary

System traps are structures that produce bad outcomes from reasonable people, which is why the intuitive fix usually deepens them. Name the trap first — drifting standards, an unowned shared resource, an unnamed opposing goal, or a proxy that has separated from the objective — because each one has a specific way out and they are not interchangeable. Drift is the one to check first, since it makes its own reference point disappear.
